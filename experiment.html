The Aldernian Experiment

Genesis

I set out to build an online AI venture. I did not expect to end up with a Crown, a constitution and a small digital country. This is how it happened — and what I am trying to find out.

Intro

I am Alan, King of the Crown Dynasty of Aldernia.

There. We may as well get that out of the way.

When I was growing up in Bristol, there was an expression occasionally deployed when somebody said something particularly unhinged: send him to Barrow Gurney.

So I appreciate that opening with the announcement that I am King of an online country does not immediately strengthen my case for continued liberty.

The important point, however, is that none of this was planned.

I did not sit down one morning and decide to create Aldernia. There was no master plan involving a Crown, a government, banks, universities, newspapers, constitutional machinery and — somewhat inevitably by this stage — a defence establishment.

I wanted to build a business.

That was it.

An online business, using artificial intelligence, which I hoped might eventually make some actual money.

I had already developed a habit of separating complicated things into different workstreams. I did not have a grand name for this. It was simply how my brain worked: if a problem had six different parts, I found it easier to treat them as six different problems and then work out how they fitted together.

ChatGPT made that habit rather more consequential.

House of Carol

The business came first.

The business became House of Carol.

It began with almost nothing: an idea, a very small amount of seed money and a fairly obvious problem.

I did not actually know how to build a company.

This turns out to be something of a disadvantage when building a company.

So I started researching.

I would use AI to help me identify a question: how do good businesses approach strategy? How should a small company think about finance? What does proper market research look like? How do serious organisations manage risk, technology, customers, quality or governance?

And then I checked.

That part matters.

I read the material. I compared sources. I went looking for authoritative guidance and examples of how competent organisations actually worked. Sometimes the AI was useful. Sometimes it was confidently wrong. Sometimes I discovered that the original question was the wrong question entirely.

None of this was instantaneous.

It could be slow, fiddly and occasionally maddening.

But it was also astonishingly fast compared with what would have been possible only a few years earlier.

I could wander into a field I knew almost nothing about, establish the shape of it, find the important sources, work out what I needed to understand and begin testing an idea against reality.

Then, inevitably, one question produced another.

A business plan produced questions about products.

Products produced questions about customers.

Customers produced questions about research, pricing, contracts and marketing.

A website produced questions about design, accessibility, privacy, security and software.

Finance wandered in.

Law followed it.

Quality assurance appeared shortly afterwards looking concerned.

And somewhere in the middle of all this I began doing something else.

Instead of asking ChatGPT simply to answer the question, I started asking it to examine the problem from different professional positions.

What would the finance person ask?

What would somebody responsible for legal risk object to?

What would a researcher want evidence for?

What would the person responsible for quality refuse to approve?

The answers still had to be checked. Giving an AI a job title does not magically send it to university.

But something useful was happening.

Different questions benefited from different ways of thinking.

So those ways of thinking began acquiring clearer boundaries.

Then responsibilities.

Then records.

Then relationships with one another.

The workstreams I had always used almost instinctively were turning into something more structured.

And this presented a wonderfully human problem.

I had built something too complicated for me to understand as an abstraction.

From organisation to ecosystem

Boxes and arrows only get you so far.

This is, I think, where the story becomes more interesting.

Human beings are remarkably good at understanding complicated systems when we can turn them into places, people and institutions.

We struggle rather more with boxes connected by arrows.

I could draw an enormous diagram showing information flows between fifty functions.

Or I could say:

That belongs to Finance.

Suddenly I knew roughly what I meant.

And once House of Carol began to resemble an organisation, another problem became impossible to ignore.

Businesses do not exist by themselves.

Neither do people.

They sit inside an extraordinary web of institutions which we mostly take for granted because, when they work properly, they are almost invisible.

BanksSchoolsUniversitiesGovernmentHospitalsCourtsNewspapersChurchesStandardsInfrastructure

Hundreds of systems quietly interacting so that somebody can open a café, get paid, catch a bus, go to university, complain about their council tax or discover that somebody somewhere has regulations concerning the permitted dimensions of a fire door.

House of Carol could not really be tested as though none of that existed.

So I wondered:

What happens if I build some of the surrounding institutional world as well?

Not real institutions, obviously.

Simulations.

Structures.

Places where different kinds of questions could live.

Government questions would go somewhere concerned with government.

Financial questions somewhere concerned with finance.

Education somewhere concerned with learning and research.

Health somewhere concerned with health.

Media somewhere capable of challenging everybody else.

And crucially, they would not all be allowed to become the same enormous all-purpose AI wearing different hats.

They would have boundaries.

The Dynasty

One thing led, rather alarmingly, to another.

Institutions appeared.

Then relationships between institutions.

Then questions about who could decide what.

Then questions about what happened when they disagreed.

Which is roughly the point at which you rediscover several thousand years of political history in miniature.

If institutions have powers, somebody has to define those powers.

If decisions are made, somebody has to record them.

If governments exist, somebody ought to be able to challenge them.

If an institution claims expertise, you need some way of asking whether it actually possesses any.

And if everything ultimately belongs to one human being sitting in Bristol with a mobile phone, that fact probably ought to appear somewhere in the constitutional arrangements.

At some point I observed, jokingly, that given the growing collection of Houses and institutions, I appeared to be King.

Unfortunately the joke was structurally useful.

So it stayed.

And that is how a man attempting to build an AI venture business accidentally acquired a Crown Dynasty.

Aldernia

Eventually I needed a world.

Where was all this happening?

The institutions were becoming coherent enough that simply describing them as separate AI projects no longer quite captured what I was doing.

They interacted.

They had histories.

They shared rules.

One institution could produce something another institution needed.

One could challenge another.

A decision made in one place could have consequences somewhere else.

What I needed was not another organisational chart.

I needed a world.

And so Aldernia emerged.

Not as a claim to sovereignty.

Not as an attempt to build an actual state.

And certainly not because I had developed territorial ambitions towards South Gloucestershire.

Aldernia is a way of making the system comprehensible.

It gives institutions somewhere to exist and something to interact with.

It allows me to test not merely whether an AI can answer a question, but whether a network of specialised, bounded, remembered and sometimes disagreeing AI-assisted institutions can help a human being do something useful.

That is a rather different question.

And I still do not know the answer.

The Experiment

So what am I actually testing?

Aldernia is part creative world, part practical laboratory and part operating environment.

Some of it is deliberately fictional. Some of it touches the real world. House of Carol, for example, is a genuine business project. Its website exists. Its business plan exists. Its finances, such as they currently are, are real.

That does not prove Aldernia works.

A website is not evidence of a functioning civilisation.

But things have crossed the boundary from conversation into reality.

And that makes the experiment worth continuing.

The question

If you give artificial intelligence something resembling institutions, responsibilities, memory, boundaries, evidence and a world in which all of those things have to interact — does it become more useful?

Perhaps.

Perhaps not.

But Aldernia now exists so that I can find out.

And, since the whole thing has already gone quite a bit further than expected, I thought I might as well keep a journal.

About

What is Aldernia?

Aldernia is an online ecosystem and experiment. It is a coherent space in which project institutions, specialist Houses, creative world-building and real-world work can interact without pretending that the fictional parts are real public authorities.

The Crown Dynasty of Aldernia is the constitutional and institutional world. House of Carol is a separate real-world business. The distinction matters, and the site says so plainly.

The point of the experiment is not to build a pretend country for its own sake. It is to create a comprehensible environment in which ideas can be tested, institutions can disagree, projects can produce real things, failures can be recorded and the whole structure can be judged by whether it is actually useful.
