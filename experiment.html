The Aldernian Experiment

What happens when a slightly exasperated conversation with ChatGPT turns into a business idea, then a set of tools, then something that begins to resemble a small working ecosystem?

What is the experiment?

The Aldernian Experiment began with no grand theory. After a particularly annoying day at work, I asked ChatGPT to help me build an AI venture business. The sensible outcome would probably have been a business plan and a list of things to do. Instead, as I worked through the problems, I kept building little tools, roles and ways of organising the work.

Some of those ideas were abstract, so I started comparing them with things that already exist in the real world: businesses, public institutions, professional roles, governance systems, quality controls and the ordinary machinery that helps groups of people get useful things done. The more I compared and tested, the more the separate pieces began to connect.

Eventually the original business stopped looking like a single project sitting in a chat window. Around it was a small ecosystem: different functions with different jobs, shared ways of working, checks and balances, records, projects and a habit of testing ideas against reality rather than simply declaring that they worked.

Most importantly, some of it escaped the realm of imagination. House of Carol became a real working business project with a functioning website and business plan. That does not prove the wider system works, but it does mean the experiment has produced tangible things.

So why Aldernia?

Aldernia is the place where I can keep testing what this strange little ecosystem might be useful for.

Part creative world, part practical laboratory and part operating environment, it gives the different pieces somewhere to interact. I can use it to support the business, organise projects and knowledge, explore how institutions and systems behave, test new ways of working, learn from the failures, and occasionally follow an idea simply because it is interesting.

The point is not to pretend that an online country is a real country. The point is to create a coherent space in which the experiment can develop, and then keep asking whether any of it survives contact with the real world.

I also want to share the journey while it is happening. Not as a polished success story written backwards after everything has worked, but as a record of the odd decisions, useful discoveries, dead ends, repairs, surprises and moments when something that sounded faintly ridiculous turns out to be unexpectedly useful.

That is what the Experiment Journal is for.
