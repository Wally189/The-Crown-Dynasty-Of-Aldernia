The Aldernian Experiment

The Hypothesis

An experiment needs something that can turn out to be wrong.

This is mine.

The main hypothesis

Working hypothesis

Artificial intelligence becomes more useful when it operates within differentiated institutions, responsibilities, memory, evidence, boundaries and human governance rather than simply as one general-purpose assistant responding to one conversation at a time.

That is the working hypothesis behind Aldernia.

It is not the conclusion.

What does “more useful” mean?

Not more impressive.

Not more verbose.

And not better at producing documents which look as though somebody important has approved them.

Useful means something harder.

Can the system produce better decisions and better work?

Can it retain relevant knowledge without continually starting again?

Can different kinds of work reach the right specialist institution?

Can one institution detect a weakness in another?

Can evidence and responsibility remain traceable?

Can work continue without the human owner becoming the memory, message bus and emergency operating system for everything?

And eventually, can any of this produce genuine value outside the experiment?

Those are more interesting tests than whether an AI can write a persuasive paragraph.

What might be true?

I suspect several things.

Specialisation may matter

An institution concerned with finance should approach a problem differently from one concerned with education, journalism or government.

If they all behave identically, the institutional structure is decorative.

Memory may matter

A system that can retrieve its previous decisions, evidence and current state should make fewer unnecessary rediscoveries and contradictions.

If it still depends on me remembering where everything is, the memory system has failed.

Boundaries may matter

Knowing what an institution may do, may not do and must escalate could make AI-assisted work more reliable.

But excessive boundaries could also make the system bureaucratic and useless.

Both outcomes are possible.

Disagreement may matter

If every institution always agrees with every other institution — particularly with me — something has probably gone wrong.

Useful disagreement may be evidence that their different responsibilities are real.

Human responsibility may matter more, not less

My suspicion is that capable AI makes human judgement more important.

Somebody still has to choose the purpose, challenge bad evidence, define authority, accept consequences and decide when not to proceed.

Aldernia is not an experiment in removing the human being.

It is partly an experiment in discovering where the human being matters most.

A world may expose things a prompt cannot

An isolated AI task can be impressive without encountering consequences.

A world creates relationships.

Decisions collide with other decisions. Institutions depend upon one another. Rules have side effects. Things acquire histories.

If Aldernia works as an experimental environment, those interactions should expose weaknesses that isolated conversations do not.

What would count against the hypothesis?

Quite a lot.

If the institutions turn out to be elaborate names for identical AI behaviour, that counts against it.

If institutional memory creates more confusion than continuity, that counts against it.

If governance grows faster than usefulness, that counts against it.

If apparent autonomy really depends on me quietly remembering everything, that counts against it.

If independent institutions merely become increasingly sophisticated ways of agreeing with their founder, that counts against it.

If internal successes repeatedly collapse when they meet reality, that matters enormously.

And if a much simpler arrangement consistently works just as well, that may be the most useful result of all.

Complexity does not win merely because I enjoyed building it.

What would support it?

Evidence that the institutional structure makes a meaningful difference.

A relevant institution retrieving the right prior knowledge without my intervention.

Two institutions identifying a problem that one alone missed.

Independent challenge changing a decision.

A mistake being caught because evidence and responsibility were traceable.

Work continuing correctly after the original conversation has disappeared into history.

A process becoming genuinely easier for the human being rather than merely moving complexity somewhere less visible.

And, eventually, real outcomes.

Someone genuinely learning something.

A real decision improving.

Useful work being adopted.

A real customer deciding that something House of Carol offers is worth paying for.

A successful simulation proves a successful simulation.

Reality has to supply its own evidence.

The wider question

Behind all of this sits a larger question:

The wider question

Can AI be deliberately organised in ways which increase its capacity to contribute to human flourishing while leaving human beings responsible for its purposes and consequences?

Aldernia will not answer the future of artificial intelligence.

It is one peculiar experiment conducted by one person with a digital country, a collection of institutions and what has become an alarming quantity of constitutional paperwork.

But perhaps it can produce useful evidence.

Perhaps some good will come from the Dynasty.

Perhaps some spectacular nonsense will come from it too.

Both are worth discovering.

The answer is not written yet.
