Common Knowledge · AI & institutions

Who is accountable when AI helps make a decision?

AI can help people search, summarise, draft, analyse and spot patterns. But the more important the decision, the more important it is to know who is responsible, what evidence was used and how a human can intervene.

Real-world explainer

The short answer

AI can assist an institution. It should not quietly become the authority.

Current UK public-sector guidance emphasises meaningful human control, clear accountability and human responsibility for AI-supported decisions. This page explains those principles; it is not legal or regulatory advice.

Assistance is not authority

A useful tool does not inherit the powers of the person using it.

AI can retrieve information, organise material, compare options, summarise text, draft language, flag patterns and support analysis. Those capabilities can be valuable. They do not, by themselves, give an AI system the professional, legal, constitutional or organisational authority held by a person or institution.

In public-sector work, current UK Government guidance is explicit that accountability does not disappear when AI is used. The Artificial Intelligence Playbook for the UK Government says organisations should have meaningful human control at the right stages and that humans should validate high-risk decisions influenced by AI. It also states that ultimate responsibility for an AI-supported output or decision rests with the public organisation using the system.

Important limit: that guidance is written for government and public-sector use. It does not settle every question of private-sector, contractual or legal liability.

Four questions

Before AI touches an important decision, make the responsibility chain visible.

  1. Who owns the decision? Name the person, role or institution that has the real authority and responsibility.
  2. What exactly may the AI do? Define the task: retrieve, summarise, compare, draft, classify, recommend or something else.
  3. Where can a competent human challenge, intervene or stop? Human oversight should be meaningful, not ceremonial.
  4. Can the organisation explain and correct what happened? Important AI use should be traceable enough for challenge, correction and redress where appropriate.

Human control

“Human in the loop” is not a magic phrase.

The Government AI Playbook recognises that human control can sit at different stages. In some systems, real-time review is appropriate. In others, human control may be designed into development, testing, monitoring, escalation and intervention rather than attached to every single output.

The key question is whether the human involvement is meaningful: does the person have enough competence, information, time and authority to notice a problem and do something about it?

The Data and AI Ethics Framework makes the same responsibility principle plain: people should remain responsible for decisions they make when those decisions are supported or informed by AI, and automated processing should include appropriate human oversight.

Transparency

Do not stop at “AI was used”.

Useful transparency explains what a tool does, why it is being used, where it affects decisions, what the limits are and who remains accountable.

The UK Government’s Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard guidance provides a structured way for public-sector bodies to publish information about algorithmic tools and why they use them.

The mandatory ATRS scope is specific. It currently covers defined central-government organisations and in-scope tools, including tools with significant influence on decisions with public effect or direct interaction with the public. It is not a universal requirement for every organisation or every use of AI.

Evidence & professional judgement

AI does not make inconvenient facts disappear.

The Civil Service Code requires civil servants to base information and advice on evidence, take decisions on the merits of the case and take due account of expert and professional advice. The Code is not an AI-governance standard, but its principle matters here: using a new tool does not remove the duties that already govern good public-service judgement.

That is a useful test for any organisation. If an AI system makes it easier to produce an answer but harder to see the evidence, challenge the reasoning or identify who is responsible, the process may have become faster without becoming better.

The Aldernian thought experiment

What if the institution itself made the boundary obvious?

Aldernia is fictional. Its institutions are designed as a public-learning world, not as real authorities.

Inside that fiction, an AI tool may assist a professional role, but it does not acquire that role’s authority merely because it can produce an answer. A system might help a fictional policy analyst compare evidence, for example, while the analyst remains responsible for the professional judgement and the authorised institution remains responsible for the decision.

The fiction is useful because it makes a normally invisible question visible: where does assistance end and authority begin?

Reality boundary: this thought experiment describes Aldernian fictional institutional design. It is not evidence that any real-world AI governance approach is effective, accredited or legally sufficient.

Practical checklist

Before relying on AI in consequential organisational work

  • Name the accountable decision owner. Do not let responsibility dissolve into “the system”.
  • Define the AI’s permitted task. Assistance should have a boundary.
  • Define the evidence and source requirements. A fluent output is not evidence by itself.
  • Identify known failure modes and uncertainty. Design around what the system can get wrong.
  • Set human review and intervention proportionately. Higher impact should normally mean stronger oversight and assurance.
  • Keep material use traceable where accountability requires it. Important decisions should be reconstructable.
  • Provide correction, escalation or challenge routes. People affected by errors should not face an unanswerable machine.
  • Use a non-AI route when AI does not earn its complexity or risk. Sometimes the best AI control is not using AI.

A final test

Could you point to the human responsibility without opening the system diagram?

If the answer is no, the governance probably needs more work.

AI can make institutions faster, more capable and more useful. But a capable institution should still be able to say, in ordinary language, who is responsible, what evidence matters and how a mistake can be challenged.
