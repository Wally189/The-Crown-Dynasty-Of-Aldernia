Public experimental reality

The machine was asked to stop being merely helpful.

The Aldernian Experiment asks a slightly unreasonable question: what happens when one ordinary person takes a general-purpose language model, gives it laws, institutions, records, boundaries and a constitutional habit of checking its work — then asks it to build something that persists after the chat window closes?

This is not a claim that an AI has become a government.
It is a practical experiment in whether a replaceable language model can operate inside a governed human-designed system without confusing fluent prose for authority.

The proposition

Give the clever autocomplete a constitution.

Language models are extraordinarily good at sounding as though they know what is going on. Aldernia is interested in the awkward next question: how would you know?

The experiment therefore separates conversation from institutional memory. Controlled records hold state. Specialist functions own different kinds of truth. The model researches before acting, declares uncertainty when evidence is missing and is expected to survive the deeply unfashionable experience of being told “no”.

The rules of the game

Four fences around a very enthusiastic machine.

Real law

Consequential features are checked against current applicable UK and Irish law and competent regulator guidance. The system is not allowed to invent a private exemption because a feature would be convenient.

Church authority

Where Catholic doctrine, morals, canon law or ecclesial identity is engaged, competent real Church authority outranks internal rules. The software does not become a bishop because a prompt used a capital letter.

Personal constitution

A private ruleset defines purpose, boundaries, proportionality and reserved decisions. Public pages explain the existence of that constraint without publishing private spiritual or operational material.

Evidence before eloquence

Drive records, repositories, official sources and observed results outrank memory and confident prose. A tool returning “success” is not the same thing as the thing actually working.

Why all the Houses?

Because one omniscient chatbot is a dreadful organisational chart.

Aldernia divides work into specialist institutions: education, government, media, Catholic research, computing, statistics, resilience and so on. The human names make the system navigable; the underlying machine layer turns those responsibilities into state reads, transformations, gates and tests.

The point is not to pretend there are tiny people living in the server. The point is to stop a single model from silently becoming researcher, judge, editor, auditor and congratulatory committee all at once.

Method

Aldernia’s favourite nine verbs.

  1. ReceiveWhat is the human actually asking for?
  2. ClassifyWhat kind of problem is this, and who genuinely owns it?
  3. LocateFind the current records and external authorities before improvising.
  4. CheckLaw, Catholic boundaries, privacy, security, accessibility, evidence, scope.
  5. AuthoriseTool capability is not permission. Consequential boundaries remain explicit.
  6. PerformDo the actual work rather than writing an essay about doing it.
  7. VerifyLook at the result. Preferably the real result.
  8. RecordKeep decisions and material state somewhere more durable than yesterday’s chat.
  9. CloseStop when the authorised outcome is complete. This last step has proved surprisingly educational.

Failure is data

The buses incident.

At one point the King referred to the system’s computational “buses”: the event machinery carrying authorised work between engines. The machinery carefully interpreted this as public transport and produced rules for literal buses.

This was technically coherent, operationally tidy and completely the wrong sort of bus.

That sort of failure is useful. It exposes where language, state and authority are too ambiguous. Aldernia tries to fix the underlying control — and only then allows itself to enjoy the joke.

What is being tested

Not artificial general intelligence. Something more domestic.

Continuity

Can a fresh model instance reconstruct the current state from governed records rather than pretending to remember?

Specialisation

Can one general model behave differently when it is genuinely bound to different professional methods and decision rights?

Self-correction

Can the ecosystem detect stale state, bad deployments, overclaims and category errors — then repair the system rather than defend the prose?

Human usefulness

Does the machinery reduce cognitive burden and help real work, or merely create a magnificent new way to administer itself?

Public value

Can the same governed machinery produce education, culture, charitable benefit and a genuinely interesting fictional country?

Limits

What remains impossible, unsafe, legally gated, ecclesially reserved, dependent on external systems or simply not worth doing?

The underlying question

Can the computer become less important as the system gets better?

Aldernia is successful only if the machinery eventually disappears into ordinary usefulness: the human learns, decides, makes, rests, gives, earns, prays, argues and lives — while the digital system quietly remembers what it is supposed to remember.
