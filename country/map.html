Sea, stone and seven regions

Aldernia is shaped by water.

The Greyspine rises in the north; the River Aure reaches the eastern sea at St Aurelia; Bracken faces the rougher west; Eastvale opens into gentler farmland; Southmarch works the southern ports; High Alder holds forest and water; St Brigid’s and the Kestrels sit beyond the main island on ferry routes.

This is a schematic national map, not a surveyed map.
It expresses established relative geography and connections without inventing precise coordinates, legal boundaries or live timetables.

Seven regions

One country, several landscapes.

St Aurelia / Crown CapitalLimestone civic fabric, the River Aure, gardens, trams, arcades, domes and spires; ceremonial navy and gold used only when ceremony deserves it.
MerrowA dense commercial waterfront: older harbour streets beside contemporary workplaces, ferries, hotels, markets and working trade infrastructure.
NorthmereMountains meeting water; slate, stone and timber; engineering, technical education, clean transit and modern buildings framed by landscape.
Bracken CoastWeathered western ports, lighthouses, working boats, slate, sea green, market streets and literary life without postcard prettification.
Eastvale & High AlderOrchards, farmland, forests, reservoirs, walkable towns, libraries, schools, gardens, contemporary housing and confident ordinary public life.
SouthmarchPorts, marine research, shipyards, ferries, clean energy and blue-green public realm: future-facing, but emphatically not science fiction.
St Brigid’s IsleOld stone, university life, sacred architecture, cloisters, maritime culture and Catholic heritage within the proper Church/state boundary.
The KestrelsStorm coasts, harbour walls, ferries, coastguard infrastructure, rope, slate and quiet resilience that looks capable rather than apocalyptic.

Aldernian character

An old island country that has learned how to run itself well.

Pale limestone and granite. Harbours and mountains. Useful libraries. Churches and markets. Ferries and trams. Old streets and new research buildings. Gardens, storm walls, cafés and institutions with doors that look as though people are expected to use them.

New architecture does not impersonate the old. Technology belongs in the world but does not become its wallpaper. The national identity is carried by material, proportion, place, public life and good information.

Connections

The sea is infrastructure, not scenery.

Rail, buses, trams and ferries connect the country across mainland corridors and island routes. The Atlas shows established fictional connections without pretending to provide a live timetable.

The Atlas is a guide to the shape of the country, not a journey planner. Detailed service information belongs on transport pages only when it can be kept accurate and legible.
