Geography

A small country with enough coastline to develop opinions.

Aldernia is an island realm formed around the principal island of Aldern, St Brigid’s Isle and the Kestrels. The west is wetter and rougher; the east gentler and more agricultural; the north climbs; the south builds ships, catches trains and occasionally goes to the beach.

The geography below is fictional canon for the founding edition, restored from retained design evidence under the current Crown commission.

Landmarks

The bones of the islands.

The Greyspine

Northern mountains, dramatic rather than Himalayan, culminating at Mount Caerwyn.

River Aure

The principal river, running through St Aurelia to a broad eastern estuary known as the Eastern Reach.

Bracken Coast

Cliffs, harbours, fishing towns, Atlantic weather and old houses that have learned what salt does to paint.

Eastvale

Fertile lowlands of orchards, farms, market towns and slow rivers.

High Alder

Wooded uplands, reservoirs, renewable energy and long walking routes.

The Kestrels

Small outer islands connected by ferries, birdlife, fishing and stubborn local competence.

Settlements

Cities big enough to disagree with one another.

St Aurelia ~760,000

The capital on the River Aure: Parliament, Crown institutions, museums, universities, government departments and neighbourhoods where none of those things are the most important event of the day.

Merrow ~820,000

The largest commercial city: docks, markets, business, culture and the useful national corrective of somewhere that considers the capital slightly over-impressed with itself.

Northmere City ~400,000

Engineering and technical education at the southern edge of the Greyspine.

Westhaven ~250,000

A western port city where fishing, arts, maritime trade and weather meet.

Bellafield ~225,000

Eastvale’s major market city and a centre for food, agriculture and regional services.

Port Avel, Dunacre & Alderfen

Southmarch industry and port life, older inland settlements, and the smaller places that prevent a national story from becoming a capital-city monologue.

Settlement populations are fictional planning baselines, not measured audience figures.

Getting around

Railways matter because the country is compact enough for them to matter.

The founding transport model assumes a national rail spine linking the principal cities, regional lines into market towns, urban buses and trams where density justifies them, ferries to St Brigid’s and the Kestrels, and roads that remain necessary without being allowed to become the entire transport policy.

Detailed timetables will only become “live” when they are backed by governed schedules rather than decorative departure boards.
